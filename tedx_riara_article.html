On a Saturday morning at the end of May, students from Muranga University of Technology were taken to something unexpected — not a lecture, not a lab, but a theatre packed with ideas that refused to stay comfortable. The event was TEDx Riara University, held at the Braeburn Theatre, and the theme — Catalysts of Change — turned out to be not just a label but a lived experience across three electric sessions.

What follows is a firsthand account of the talks, the ideas, and the questions that are still rattling around three days later.

Session 01 The Big Question What if you are wrong?
Dr. Doris Change
Thank God It's Monday Again

Founder, Elite Governance and Leadership Center · PhD Strategic Leadership · Author

Dr. Doris opened the event with a provocation most of us had never considered: people don't dread Monday mornings because of the work — they dread them because of the leadership around them. Her framework, ELITE, reframes what good leadership looks like from the ground up.

She challenged the room to stop waiting for a job title before showing leadership. Influence, trust, and emotional intelligence are not rewards for seniority — they are the tools you start using now.

The ELITE Framework
EExecute Presence — show up fully, not just physically
LLeadership Visibility — be seen doing the work
IInfluence Before Title — lead before you're given permission
TTrust and Emotional Intelligence — the invisible architecture of great teams
EEngage and Empower — build organizations people actually want to show up for
Moses Kakoa
Strategic Communication

Strategic Communications & Partnerships Lead, Cause Vision

Moses began with something disarmingly personal: his relationship with his mother and the endless arguments of being the last-born. Their resolution? A pact — before any argument escalates, both sides must fully articulate the other's perspective first.

That one household rule became the lens for a much larger idea about how we communicate across generations, cultures, and contexts. He spoke about the power of being someone who can translate between worlds — especially in tech, where teams are built of people from radically different backgrounds. That difference, he argues, is not a problem to manage. It is the product.

"Africa is not behind. Africa is rising. And I am part of the voice making that undeniable."

Key Frameworks
Reality = both sides holding their perceptions simultaneously, not one overwriting the other
Working across generations and ethnicities is a competitive advantage, not a diversity checkbox
The future belongs to those who can translate — between disciplines, cultures, and eras
Kamau Munyori
Are You Cringing More Lately?

Strategy Planning Lead, Ogilvy Africa · MSc Consumer Analytics, University of Leeds

This was perhaps the most memorable talk of the first session. Kamau's central insight: the discomfort you feel when encountering a new idea — that cringe, that visceral "this is wrong" — is not a signal to retreat. It might be a signal that you're standing at the edge of the future.

His examples hit hard. When M-Pesa was first introduced, finance professionals laughed. You want to keep all your money on a Nokia button phone? Transactions that take days in a bank, done by SMS? Ridiculous. Today, M-Pesa is the backbone of the Kenyan economy and a model studied the world over.

Then came Birkenstock. First sold in Germany in 1963 and dismissed as "ugly health footwear," it ended up being imported to the US through health food stores. The counterculture loved it precisely because mainstream fashion rejected it. By the 1990s, Kate Moss was wearing them in fashion shoots. Today, Birkenstock collaborates with Dior, Valentino, and Manolo Blahnik.

The pattern is consistent: early discomfort is not evidence of a bad idea. It is often evidence of a new one.

The Central Question
When something makes you cringe, ask yourself honestly: is it a bad idea — or just an unfamiliar one?
Collective judgment now moves faster than culture can absorb. Cringe is often the gap.
The world's most transformative ideas almost always began as someone else's punchline

"Early discomfort is not evidence of a bad idea. It is often evidence of a new one."

— Kamau Munyori, Strategy Planning Lead, Ogilvy Africa
Session 02 Permission Denied Why are you waiting?
Moses Liech
Converting Thoughts Into Action

Founder & CEO, Investa Farm — Fintech, UK & Kenya

Moses runs a fintech company that turns farm assets into tradeable investment classes. His talk stripped away the myth of the perfect plan. The insight: clarity is not a precondition for action. It is a byproduct of it.

His framework, R3, offers a sequence for anyone sitting on an idea that feels too big to begin.

The R3 Framework
RRecognize — awareness is always the first step. Name the thing you keep avoiding.
RReduce — shrink the scope. Run a small test. Learn with low risk and high feedback.
RRelief — the weight lifts when you start moving. Action creates momentum, not the other way around.
Lines Worth Keeping
"Clarity comes from action."
"Builders don't wait for permission. They create momentum."
Samuel Obukosia
AI & Entrepreneurship in Africa

Director, i3 Launchpad · Startup Ecosystem Architect · AI Innovator

Samuel's question was blunt and necessary: when you use AI to build something, are you downloading someone else's solution — or are you actually solving a problem that exists in your context?

His work at i3 Launchpad supports young African founders through programs like i3Forge — not by handing them tools, but by helping them understand what problem they are actually trying to solve before they touch a single line of code.

His challenge: Africa should not be a consumer of technology. It should be a creator of it. But that requires building from context, not from imported assumptions.

The Core Challenge
"Are we downloading a solution — or solving a problem?"
Context matters. The same technology produces completely different outcomes depending on who deploys it and for whom.
O'Maxwell Gad
Decentralizing Opportunity

CEO, The O'Gad Impact Group

O'Maxwell challenged the invisible gravity that pulls every entrepreneur toward Nairobi, toward Lagos, toward the capital. His argument: the most underserved problems — and therefore the largest opportunities — are not in the cities. They are in the places no one is looking.

Agriculture. Rural logistics. Informal trade networks. These are not niche plays. They are the foundation of the African economy, and they are waiting for builders who are willing to go there.

The Opportunity Map
Not all value is in cities. The highest-impact opportunities often sit in the places with the least competition.
Decentralization is not just a blockchain concept — it's a strategy for inclusive economic growth.
Mary Mwangi
Fintech & Mass Transit

Co-Founder & CEO, Data Integrated Limited · Board Member, Association of Fintechs in Kenya

Mary's company has spent 12 years turning the chaos of African mass transit into a technology-first sector. Her talk was a masterclass in identifying where the real power sits — not in boardrooms, but in the everyday friction that millions of people experience moving around a city.

She traced her fintech idea from observation to startup, emphasizing the importance of sitting at the intersection of a genuine problem and technical capacity. The World Economic Forum, Citibank's Tech for Integrity, and Google Launchpad have all recognized the impact.

Key Insight
"Power sits where the reality is." Go to where the actual problem lives — not where the comfortable conversation happens.
Session 03 What Are the Things We Don't Say The unspeakable truths
Samson Ngengi Njuguna
Intellectual Property & Dignity

Director, Digital Transformation, Office of the First Lady, Statehouse · Managing Director, Geoarms Systems Limited

This was the talk that landed heaviest. Samson is not just a geospatial systems expert and doctoral researcher — he is also someone who has lived the cost of building something valuable without protection. His story of IP theft, of a brother lost to depression from a similar experience, of fighting through loans and legal systems to protect not just himself but every young innovator who comes after him — it was delivered without performance, and that made it hit harder.

Twenty years at the frontier of digital transformation, from the Kenya Revenue Authority to UNDP and FAO mandates, and his most urgent message was not about systems. It was about identity. About not disappearing inside someone else's machine.

Words That Stay
"Don't complain about problems. Build solutions."
"Don't disappear in your creativity. Find yourself."
"Don't be a ghost in someone else's system." — Employment that extracts your genius without credit is not a career, it's a transaction you're losing.
"Protection is the alignment with dignity."
"Protect your own life."
James Nyabera
Men's Mental Health

Founder, A Man Must Cry Mental Health Movement · Africa CDC Mental Health Leadership Changemaker

James holds two roles that don't often appear in the same sentence: research scientist and lived-experience mental health advocate. His movement — A Man Must Cry — exists to dismantle the cultural architecture that tells men that letting go, grieving, crying, and asking for help are signs of weakness.

His message was simple and necessary: releasing is not breaking down. It is how you rebuild. Whether you are a man or not, the relief of letting something go is not surrender. It is the precondition for showing up whole again.

Loraine (Lolo) Sagini
When Do You Stop Being Yourself?

Digital Marketer, Project Manager & Content Creator

Lolo told the truth about university that most people only speak in private — the way it can swallow you whole, the way depression can settle in so quietly that you don't notice you've stopped recognizing yourself in the mirror. She credited her family with helping her find her way back.

Her talk was about identity as a living thing — not fixed, not given, always evolving. And the courage required not just to be yourself, but to keep choosing yourself in environments that reward conformity.

Lines That Linger
"Change becomes impossible when you stop pretending." — You have to name the thing before you can change it.
"Growth is visible when we look back." — You cannot always see it in the moment. That's okay.
Silvia Muhaya
Dignity Over Dependency

CEO, Aletheia Institute · Advocate of the High Court of Kenya

Silvia arrives at empowerment through the law — with precision, analysis, and a structured critique of the ways well-meaning interventions can create the very dependency they claim to fight. Her work with Aletheia Institute focuses on equipping young people and entrepreneurs not with resources, but with the capacity to think, decide, and build independently.

The distinction she draws is sharp: real empowerment is not the transfer of resources. It is the cultivation of the skills, confidence, and ownership required to thrive without reliance on someone else's generosity.

Maria Angelica Mejia
Urban Biodiversity & Citizen Action

Curator for Latin America & Caribbean, The Nature of Cities · The Cerros de Bogotá Foundation

Maria closed the event with a view from Bogotá — where she is working to consolidate the city's eastern hills into the largest urban socio-ecological corridor in Latin America: 57 kilometres of living infrastructure threading through one of the continent's largest cities.

Her message was unexpected and landed well in a room full of tech-focused builders: find the people who see what you see. Start small. The tribe matters more than the scale. And protect what is valuable — not just because it is yours, but because it serves others.

The Builder's Takeaway
Find your tribe before you find your scale. Community engagement is collective learning, not just outreach.
Protect what is rightful and useful — in ecology, in innovation, in life, this is the same principle.

"Don't be a ghost in someone else's system. Protect your own life."

— Samson Ngengi Njuguna, Director of Digital Transformation, Office of the First Lady

// Closing Reflection

Sitting in that theatre, what became clear across three sessions and twelve voices was that the theme — Catalysts of Change — was not about change as a distant event. It was about change as something that begins in a decision made before anyone gives you permission to make it.

Dr. Doris says lead before you have the title. Moses Kakoa says listen to the other side before you insist you're right. Kamau says the idea that makes you cringe might be the one that matters. Moses Liech says start before you're ready. Samuel says build from context, not from imports. O'Maxwell says go where no one is looking. Samson says protect what you build.

James says let yourself feel it. Lolo says stay yourself even when the system asks you not to. Silvia says real empowerment is never handed to you. Maria says find your tribe.

Twelve talks. One throughline: you are already the catalyst. The question is what you're waiting for.
